Idée centrale

Session 1 ended on: society is constructed, the social order could be otherwise. How is it held together? Through shared meaning: myths, rites and doctrines — and so do companies. “Myth founds. Rites enact. Doctrine fixes. Remove any one and meaning destabilizes.”

01IntroductionWhy anthropology for managers

Object of anthropology

The surplus over the useful: graduations, weddings, integration weekends or conventions (costumes, repeated words, precise order, photographs) are not “useful” in a technical sense, yet the group feels something essential is missing without them. Humans don’t merely act: they give their actions meaning. Anthropology = “the science of human meaning”; beneath every “obvious” practice lies a symbolic system.

3 reasons it matters

  • A firm is not only a value machine: it is a community of meaning; people commit or quit largely for symbolic reasons.
  • Strategy and change fail more often on culture (meaning) than on spreadsheets (technique).
  • Anthropology gives the rarest skill: reading and shaping your organization’s symbolic order deliberately.

Key questions: Why do humans need myths? How do rites structure collective life? What role does doctrine play in stabilizing meaning?

Part 1 takeaway

Humans are meaning-seeking, belonging-seeking, symbol-making animals. Societies cohere not by force or interest alone but by shared meaning.

Myth

A myth is not a “false story”

A foundational narrative that gives a group its origin, identity and direction. Its truth is not factual but meaning-bearing. Myths answer what data never can: where do we come from, who are we, where are we going? They are the deep code of identity. Hook: strip a company of its founding story and its “values” become empty slogans.

  • Eliade: myths tell of the origin (“in the beginning”), anchoring the present in a sacred founding moment that rituals periodically re-actualize; to found is to give a cosmogony. Hook: anniversaries and “founding day” rituals.
  • Lévi-Strauss: myth is a machine for resolving contradictions a society cannot otherwise resolve (life/death, nature/culture); meaning lies in the structure of relations, not the surface plot (“myths think themselves in men”). Hook: a corporate saga often reconciles profit vs purpose.
  • Barthes (Mythologies, 1957): myth moved into advertising, brands and mass culture; its trick is turning the cultural/historical into the “natural” and self-evident (the new Citroën as a modern goddess). Myth is a type of speech that hides its own construction — exactly what a critical manager must learn to see through. Hook: a brand myth makes a commercial promise feel like a natural truth.
  • Girard: groups are bound by mimetic desire (we want what others want) ⇒ rivalry ⇒ crisis resolved by designating a scapegoat whose expulsion restores unity; founding myths often conceal this founding violence. Beneath unity there is sometimes an expelled victim; recognizing the pattern is “moral lucidity”. Hook: after a crisis, watch for the “scapegoat executive” sacrificed to restore calm.

Rites

Rite

A codified, repeated symbolic action that enacts shared meaning. If myth is the story, the rite is the performance that makes it real and renews it. Four functions: 1. integration (we belong) · 2. cohesion (we are one) · 3. legitimacy (this is official) · 4. safe transition (change without chaos). Hook: you cannot transmit culture by memo, only by repeated, embodied rites.

  • van Gennep (1909): every change of status follows 3 phases — separation (you leave the old status) · liminality (the in-between threshold) · reincorporation (you enter the new status). Birth, initiation, marriage, retirement. Transitions are dangerous; rites carry people across safely and visibly. Hook: onboarding and promotions — design the three phases.
  • Durkheim: gathered in ritual, individuals feel a shared energy, collective effervescence, fusing them into a moral community. Religion = society worshipping itself; rites renew the bond and recharge the sacred (set apart from the profane). Hook: the annual convention is engineered effervescence.
  • Goffman: daily life is full of micro-rites (greetings, “face-work”, deference, demeanor) protecting each person’s dignity and keeping the interaction order running. Hook: the standup, Monday meeting, after-work drink.

Doctrine

Doctrine

The explicit, codified body of belief and rule: “what we hold true and how one must behave”; it tells members what is allowed, forbidden and valued. It stabilizes meaning: the group doesn’t re-decide everything daily; without it, myth and rite drift. Hook: a values charter no one can violate is doctrine; one with no teeth is decoration.

  • Douglas (How Institutions Think): institutions encode categories and classifications that quietly do our thinking for us; doctrine is the institutionalized grid of what is even thinkable.
  • Bourdieu: doxa = the taken-for-granted, never discussed · orthodoxy = official defended belief · heterodoxy = challenge & dissent. Use it: locate any internal debate as doxa (unspoken), orthodoxy (official line) or heterodoxy (dissent).

Cas de classe 1 — The company that values “speak up” (pas de corrigé)

The company officially values Courage · Transparency · Speak Up. A young manager publicly challenges the CEO’s strategy with well-documented arguments; the CEO reacts badly; six months later the manager is quietly removed from the leadership program. Question: what is the doxa? the orthodoxy? the heterodoxy? What is the company doctrine? The cours gives no official answer. (Use Bourdieu’s three definitions above to structure your reasoning.)

04Partie 3Transposition to organizations

A company is a modern society, with its own myths, rites and doctrines.

ArtifactsThe visible: offices, logos, dress, rituals
Espoused valuesThe stated: values charter, mission, slogans
Basic assumptionsThe invisible, taken-for-granted

The iceberg: the real culture lives below the waterline; change that ignores the bottom level fails.

Takeaway: the anthropological triptych is a ready-made diagnostic grid for any organization.

Corporate myths

The garage, the kitchen table, the first improbable sale: companies tell a sacred origin (Eliade), starring a hero (Campbell), that charters the present (Malinowski). The founder myth scripts behaviour long after the founder is gone. People commit to stories, not processes; every onboarding should transmit the myth, not just the org chart.

Exemple du cours — IKEA (myth & doctrine)

The thrifty farm boy Ingvar Kamprad; the flat-pack “discovery”; frugality elevated into a sacred mission. The origin myth still scripts behaviour decades on and is fixed in writing as doctrine: The Testament of a Furniture Dealer (1976), a literal corporate scripture of frugality and purpose that new managers still study.

Exemple du cours — Michelin (corporate mythology)

Bibendum (the tyre man, 1898); the heroic age of the automobile; the paternalist company town of Clermont-Ferrand — a dense mythology binding a region and a workforce. Doctrine of taste: the Michelin Guide and its stars (from 1900) became an orthodoxy of gastronomy, defining what “excellence” means for a whole field.

Exemple du cours — Apple keynote (rite & effervescence)

The staged launch — darkened hall, countdown, “one more thing”, collective applause — is engineered Durkheimian effervescence: thousands fused into one believing community. It re-actualizes the founder myth (Jobs) and recharges belonging; remove it and the bond quietly cools.

Exemple du cours — Strategic doctrines

Values charters, codes of conduct, mission/vision statements make doctrine explicit: “The Toyota Way”, the old “HP Way”, Amazon’s Leadership Principles. Doctrine converts a founder’s charisma into something transmissible (Weber’s routinization: the bridge from a person to an institution). If your “values” can’t settle a real trade-off, they’re decoration, not doctrine.

Exemple du cours — Danone (doctrine as contested power)

Under the French loi PACTE (2019) Danone enshrined a social-environmental purpose (entreprise à mission) as corporate doctrine. In 2021 CEO Emmanuel Faber was ousted amid investor pressure: a collision of two doctrines, stakeholder purpose vs shareholder value. Doctrine is never neutral: it is power, and it is contested.

Cas de classe 2 — Apple, Michelin, Huawei, Patagonia (pas de corrigé)

Each group chooses a company and completes: Myth (what story does it tell about itself?) · Hero (who embodies it?) · Rites (how is belonging enacted?) · Doctrine (what beliefs and rules are non-negotiable?) · Symbols (what objects, language, places carry meaning?) · Taboos (what would be culturally unacceptable?). Then: “Could another company copy these practices and obtain the same culture?” — no official answer in the slides.

The stance to hold

Use meaning to build genuine community; refuse to use it to manipulate.

Cas de classe 3 — The amazing culture (pas de corrigé)

A fast-growing tech company: employees call themselves a “family”; intensive three-day onboarding retreat; the founder’s story repeated constantly; company merchandise; regular celebrations; strong public social purpose; extremely high engagement scores — but employees who publicly challenge the founder rarely remain long. Question: strong culture or cult-like culture? (Link: myth, rites, doctrine, heterodoxy being punished.)

Synthesis — the spine of the session

Humans are symbol-making animals → societies cohere through shared meaning → meaning is organized as Myth · Rites · Doctrine → organizations are no exception: read & shape it, ethically. Outcome: decode the invisible culture of any organization.
