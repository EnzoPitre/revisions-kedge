Economics — The Major Schools of Thought

Sociology of Social Transformations & Management · Session 3

13 minFicheNo neutral definition of economics, the five schools (classical, Marxist, neoclassical, Keynesian, critiques), consequences for management (incentives, game theory, shareholder value) and the meta-lesson.

Idée centrale

Economics is not one truth: it is a field of rival theories with political consequences. Each school is a different bet on human nature, markets and the state. Management is applied economics: your (often unspoken) school decides how you lead.

01IntroductionWhy economics for managers

Where we left off: Session 2 ended on Mauss: exchange began with the gift, not price; the market is one historical form of exchange, not a law of nature. Today’s question: what is “the economy”, and why can brilliant economists read the same facts and reach opposite conclusions?

3 reasons it matters

  1. Every tool you’ll use (KPIs, bonuses, transfer prices, “shareholder value”, outsourcing…) is applied economic theory. “Use it blind, and the theory uses you.”
  2. Models are performative: believed widely enough, they reshape the world to fit themselves.
  3. The educated manager chooses the lens consciously.

Key questions: What is the economy really about? Why do economists disagree? How do economic ideas shape management?

02Partie 1What is economics? No neutral definition

“How you define it already smuggles in a worldview.”

DefinitionContent
Robbins (1932): scarcityHuman behaviour as a relation between ends and scarce means with alternative uses. Mainstream view: the universal logic of allocation under rarity
Aristotle: oikonomia vs chrematistikēOikonomia = managing the household for a good life (natural limit). Chrematistikē = money-making for its own sake (limitless), judged unnatural. The 2,400-year-old question: is the economy for life, or life for the economy?

What economics studies

Classic circuit: production → exchange → distribution → consumption. How each step works and who gets what is where schools disagree. “Distribution” is where politics enters economics: who gets the value created is a choice, not a law. Hook: a firm is a small economy: it produces, exchanges and distributes value.

Question de classe (pas de corrigé)

A product is sold for €100. How should the value be distributed between employees, suppliers, shareholders, government, investment, communities, environment?

The myth of neutrality

  • Positive economics describes what is; normative economics argues what ought to be. The line is real but porous: every model embeds values (what counts as welfare, whose preferences, what is “rational”).
  • Performativity: taught widely, a model makes the world behave as it predicts — it helps produce the reality it claims to describe.

Hook: adopting a metric quietly imposes the theory behind it on your whole organization.

03Partie 2The five schools (the map)

“A timeline of arguments, not a museum. Each assumes a different human, market and state.”

SchoolStartsKey words
Classical1776→invisible hand, free markets

Neoclassical1870→homo œconomicus, optimization

Classical

  • Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776): division of labour (pin factory) multiplies productivity; self-interest led “as if by an invisible hand” to benefit society; markets coordinate without a planner. Crucial nuance: Smith was a moral philosopher (Theory of Moral Sentiments) who grounded markets in sympathy and trust, not greed. Hook: specialization and competition still structure strategy but markets need morals to work.
  • Ricardo — comparative advantage: even a country worse at everything gains by specializing where it is relatively best; the enduring case for free trade (still supports globalization and supply chains). Say — “Say’s Law”: production generates the income to buy it, so general gluts are impossible; he put the entrepreneur at the centre. Hook: “focus on your relative strength” is Ricardo; “build it and demand will come” is Say (and often dangerously wrong).

Marxist

  • Marx, Capital (1867): workers produce more value than their wage; the surplus value captured by capital is exploitation; value comes from labour and its distribution is a relation of power. Whatever one’s politics, Marx forced distribution, conflict and power to the centre. Hook: “who captures the surplus along our value chain?” is a live strategic question.

Neoclassical

  • Marginal revolution (1870s): Jevons, Menger and Walras, independently, based value not on labour but on marginal utility; economics rebuilt around the individual chooser. Foundation of every demand curve and pricing model. Hook: “price to the value of the next unit to the customer” is pure marginalism.
  • Walras (general equilibrium: all markets clearing at once), Pareto (efficiency: no one better off without someone worse off), Marshall (toolkit: supply and demand, elasticity, the margin, short vs long run). Powerful but idealized; Pareto efficiency says nothing about fairness. Hook: every pricing analysis is Marshall’s scissors; beware “win-win”.
  • Homo œconomicus: rational, self-interested, optimizing, stable preferences and (often) full information; social outcomes explained by aggregating such agents. Dominant in business schools and policy: elegant, tractable, “often wrong about real humans”. Hook: most optimization tools assume it.
  • Hayek (Austrian turn) — the knowledge problem: no central planner can gather the dispersed, local knowledge that prices silently coordinate; the market is a spontaneous order. The deepest case for markets is information, not greed. Hook: over-centralized control destroys the local knowledge that prices and front-line teams hold.
  • Friedman (Chicago): monetarism, faith in free markets, and the 1970 doctrine that “the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits.” Root of shareholder-value management; reshaped governance for fifty years, now fiercely contested. Hook: “maximize shareholder value” is one contested doctrine, not neutral common sense.

Keynesian

  • Keynes, The General Theory (1936): economies can get stuck in involuntary unemployment when aggregate demand is too low; under radical uncertainty investment swings on animal spirits; the state can stabilize demand. Key terms: effective demand (spending drives output & jobs), animal spirits (confidence drives investment), the multiplier (one euro spent ripples outward). Hook: investment, hiring and M&A ride on confidence as much as on spreadsheets.

Contemporary critiques

CritiqueIdeaManagerial hook
Behavioral (Herbert Simon)Assault on homo œconomicus: bounded rationality (we “satisfice”); people are not rational optimizers but predictably irrationalDesign for the real humans you have, not the textbook ones
Commons (Hardin → Ostrom)Hardin: shared resources are doomed (“tragedy of the commons”). Elinor Ostrom (first woman Nobel in economics) refuted the inevitability: communities govern commons successfully under identifiable design principles of governanceShared data, codebases, brand reputation and culture are commons: govern them by Ostrom’s principles
Sen: capability approachDevelopment = expansion of real freedoms: what people are actually able to do and be, not merely income or utilityMeasure what enables people to flourish, not only revenue
Piketty: inequality returnsWhen the return on capital exceeds growth (r > g), wealth concentrates structurally over time; inequality is a structural tendencyExecutive-pay ratios and wealth concentration are strategic reputational issues
Raworth (2021): Doughnut EconomySocial foundation (inner boundary: energy, equality, nutrition, decent work) and ecological ceiling (outer boundary: climate, biodiversity, deforestation, chemical pollution)A company cannot thrive for long in a world that is “failing”

Each school is a different bet on human nature, markets and the state. None is the whole truth — and that is the point.

04Partie 3Consequences for management
TopicIdeaHook
Shareholder-value doctrineManagers are self-interested agents, aligned with owners via stock-based pay; the firm’s purpose = maximize shareholder return. Challenged by stakeholder and commons views (Ostrom), the entrepreneurial state and ESG / “raison d’être”. Callback: Danone & Faber (2021), a literal clash of shareholder value vs stakeholder purpose“What is performance?” has no neutral answer
Agency theory & incentive payNeoclassical default: people respond to monetary incentives, so align the agent (manager/employee) with the principal (owner): bonuses, stock options, commissions. Assumes homo œconomicus and can produce perverse effectsA metric tied to a bonus will be optimized, including in ways you never intended
When money backfiresCrowding-out: paying for blood donations reduced supply (money destroyed the gift’s meaning). Deci & Ryan: extrinsic rewards can crowd out intrinsic motivation. Daycare: fining late parents increased lateness: the fine turned a duty into a priceBefore pricing a behaviour, ask whether you’ll destroy the goodwill that sustained it. “A bonus can buy effort and kill commitment”
Game theory & coopetitionPrisoner’s dilemma: individually rational choices can leave everyone worse off. Axelrod’s tournaments: in repeated games cooperative “tit-for-tat” wins, hence “coopetition”. Compete or cooperate depends on one-shot vs repeated game; most business is repeatedReputation and reciprocity beat one-shot exploitation
Mondragon (Spain) & CoaseFederation of worker-owned cooperatives, tens of thousands of members across industry, retail and finance: shared ownership and cooperation can run large competitive firms (like an Ostrom-style commons in corporate form). Coase: the firm itself is an island of planned cooperation inside the market; markets are not the only efficient way to coordinateCooperation is an organizational design choice, not a utopia

Cas 2 — The €50 million decision (pas de corrigé)

Unexpected €50m profit. Options: A return to shareholders · B raise wages and bonuses · C invest in automation and AI · D reduce prices · E invest in decarbonization · F build cash reserves. Question: what is the “right” economic decision? The meta-lesson below is the intended takeaway: no neutral answer.

The meta-lesson: why economists disagree

  • Different assumptions about rationality, the role of the state and what counts as value. Change the assumption and the conclusion flips.
  • Different values: efficiency vs equality vs freedom vs sustainability. “Mill’s point returns: distribution is a human choice.”
  • Performativity: models help produce the reality they describe. Economics is plural and political: a set of lenses, not a single mirror.

The spine of the session

No neutral definition of the economy → rival schools assume different humans, markets & states → management is applied economics → choosing your lens (consciously, ethically) is leadership. Outcome: economics is plural & political, not a single truth.

Next stage & final-exam question

Session 4 — Philosophy: behind every economic school lies a vision of the human being and the good life (Smith’s sympathy, Marx’s emancipation, homo œconomicus, Sen’s freedom). Session 3’s ingredient: AI optimizes within a model — choosing and judging the model is human.

Final-exam question (keep it in mind all courses): “Why are the humanities essential for future managers in the age of artificial intelligence?”
