Idée centrale

We buy and consume many products because we want to highlight or hide some aspects of the self (feel successful, attractive, connect with a loved one). Consumers’ feelings about themselves shape their consumption practices — this is where Session 1’s symbolic consumption plugs into who we are (from symbolic to conspicuous consumption). Brands can turn this into customer experience through the self-image congruence model.

01ConceptSelf-concept & self-esteem

Self-concept

The beliefs a person holds about his/her own attributes, and how he/she evaluates the self on these qualities.

DimensionMeaning (slide)
Contente.g. facial attractiveness vs mental aptitude
Positivityself-esteem
Intensity & stabilityover time
Accuracyself-assessment vs reality

Consumers’ self-assessments can be quite distorted, especially regarding physical appearance (slide example: “Snapchat dysmorphia”).

The self is malleable

We have as many selves as different social roles. Multiple selves: depending on the situation we behave differently, use different products and vary how we display ourselves. Role identities (dramaturgical perspective): only some are active at a given time (mother, worker, friend…); the dominant role shapes behaviour (dress, speech, attitude).

Self-esteem

The positivity of a person’s self-concept: the need to feel good about ourselves in our various roles.

Self-concept theory: we have a concept of who we think we are (actual / real self: realistic appraisal of the qualities we do and don’t have) and who we’d like to be (ideal self). The bigger the gap, the lower the self-esteem. → We buy products consistent with our actual self and products that help us reach an ideal standard.

02Les autresImpression management & social comparison

Impression management (Goffman, 1959)

We work hard to “manage” what others think of us, presenting ourselves the way an actor performs for an audience. Theatrical metaphor: individuals select costumes, props and settings to project a desired identity.

Front stageBack stage
In public: plays a role and carefully manages their imageIn private: vigilance relaxes; behaves more naturally

Slide images: a job interview and an Instagram grid (front stage); “Facebook version of you vs the realistic version” (back stage).

Social comparison theory (Festinger, 1954)

People evaluate their own appearance and worth by comparing themselves with others, especially in the absence of objective standards.

TypeDescription
UpwardComparing to someone better off: can motivate improvement — or spark envy and a sense of inadequacy
DownwardComparing to someone less fortunate: often boosts self-esteem, sometimes at the cost of guilt
LateralComparing to peers similar in status or skill: the most common everyday benchmark
  • Self-esteem: constant exposure to high-achieving peers can lower mood and confidence.
  • Motivation: healthy benchmarking can push consumers to study harder, train more, pursue goals.
  • Digital behaviour: social media structurally favours upward comparison (users mostly post curated, idealised moments).
03NumériqueThe digital self & new ways to express the self

Digital environments let us construct multiple versions of the self.

FormContent
On social mediaLinkedIn (professional self), Instagram (aesthetic self), Facebook (social self): each platform stages a different version of you, anchored to your real-life identity
As avatarsSocial media, gaming and VR let users assume virtual identities (realistic doubles to stylized, superhero-like characters). Consumers buy virtual goods (skins, outfits) to customize them

Anti-consumption as self-defining

Individuals may deliberately avoid certain products, practices or brands in order not to be associated with social groups their peers find undesirable. Avoiding can be as important as seeking. Boycotting or avoiding products can be a form of self-expression and emancipation. Slide image: “3 reasons to boycott Shein”.

Embodied cognition

Bodily states influence mental states. Physical experiences and interactions with products (wearing certain clothes, adopting particular postures, using a product) can shape how individuals perceive themselves and express their identity. Slide image: trail-running equipment.

04BelkThe extended self

Extended self (Belk, 1988)

The external objects, possessions, and even people or places that individuals consider part of themselves: belongings that help define one’s identity and self-concept.

  • Roots — William James (1890): the Self is the sum of all we call ours (body, family, reputation, land, even money). Belk turned this into a testable marketing construct.
  • Losing possessions = losing self: burglary and disaster victims often grieve like mourning a loved one (a bicycle-theft victim said the thief stole “a piece of my life”).
  • Slide images: Nike By You (customized sneakers), Héloïse & Abélard (circular fine jewelry), football supporters.

05Composante 1Personality

Personality

A person’s unique psychological makeup and how it consistently influences the way a person responds to his/her environment: a unique combination of inner characteristics (traits) that reflects individual differences and remains consistent and enduring over time.

Marketers cannot change consumers’ personalities to meet their products, but understanding which traits drive the desired response helps them appeal to the target audience.

TheoryEra (slide)Core idea
Psychoanalytic (Freud)1900sPersonality stems from unconscious conflicts
Neo-Freudian1930s–1950sPersonality shaped by social relationships, not just instincts
Trait theory1940s–presentPersonality as a set of measurable, stable traits

Freud & psychoanalytic theory

Personality stems from a conflict between the desire to gratify physical needs (Id, pleasure principle) and the need to function as a responsible member of society (Superego, the person’s conscience).

ComponentPrincipleRole
Id (“ça”)PleasureImmediate gratification, “party animal” of the mind, no regard for consequences
EgoRealityMediates between id and superego to find realistic solutions
SuperegoMoralityCounterweight to the id: conscience, internalizing society’s rules

In consumer research: the Id points to unconscious motives guiding purchases; the Ego relies on symbolism in the product to reconcile them with the Superego’s constraints. Motivational research borrows these ideas to uncover deeper meanings behind products and ads (we channel socially unacceptable needs into acceptable outlets), mainly through depth interviews.

Exemple du cours — motivational research

“Acceptable” motiveAssociated productsUnconscious motive
Femininity(Baking) cakes and cookiesGiving birth
MasculinityCars, red meat, shaving with a razorExpressing power, virility
Social acceptanceSugar and honeyExpressing love and affection
IndividualityGourmet foods, cars, perfumesShowing how unique you are

Neo-Freudian theories

Social variables, not biological instincts, are the main force shaping personality (ongoing interdependence between individual and society).

Jung — collective unconscious & archetypes

Personality is shaped in part by the collective unconscious, a shared reservoir of universal archetypes (inherited symbolic patterns beyond individual experience). Brands often embody a specific archetype, recognisable across cultures and reinforced in tone, visuals and storytelling. Slide wheel: e.g. Apple = Creator, Dove = Innocent, Google = Sage, North Face = Explorer, Harley-Davidson = Rebel, Disney = Magician, Nike = Hero, Chanel = Lover, M&M’s = Jester, IKEA = Everyman, WWF = Nurturer, Rolex = Ruler.

Horney — three interpersonal orientations (CAD)

Personality develops mainly to manage the anxiety born from relationships with others.

OrientationDirectionNeedsSlide logo
CompliantMoving toward othersLove, approval, belonging; seeks affection and acceptanceMcDonald’s
AggressiveMoving against othersPower, success, admiration; relationships through competitionOn
DetachedMoving away from othersIndependence, self-sufficiency; avoids commitment, prefers distanceBeats

CAD scale (Cohen, 1967) to segment consumers: compliant types favour well-known, socially reassuring brands; aggressive types favour status/performance products; detached types favour low-interaction goods.

Trait theory

Traits

Personality = predispositional attributes called traits: a quantitative approach, in sharp contrast with Freud. A trait = any distinguishable, relatively enduring way in which one individual differs from another (e.g. innovativeness, materialism, self-consciousness, need for cognition). Unlike Freud’s clinical interpretation (depth interviews), traits are measured directly through standardized, multi-item scales.

06Composante 2The body: body image & ideals of beauty

Body image & ideal of beauty

Body image = a consumer’s subjective evaluation of his/her physical self. Our satisfaction depends on how closely we think it corresponds to the ideal of beauty in our culture. An ideal of beauty = a particular model or exemplar of appearance (physical features, clothing styles, cosmetics, hairstyles, skin tone, body type).

  • Ideals evolved with time and depend on culture (slide: 1950s wide hips and full bust · 1960s ultra-slim without curves · 2000s skinny but athletic · 2010s small waist with full curves) and differ by gender.
  • Our desire to match these ideals drives many purchase decisions (cosmetics, fashion, fitness, cosmetic surgery).
  • Advertising and mass/social media play a significant role in what we consider desirable and can lower self-esteem when consumers compare themselves to these ideals.
  • Question posed on the slide: “And what about men? Are today’s beauty ideals placing just as much pressure on men as on women?” (no answer given).
Body modificationBody positivity
Altering the body itself to conform more closely to the ideal (tattoos, piercings, dieting, exercise, cosmetic surgery; e.g. teeth whitening, weight-loss programs — slide image: WeightWatchers)Rejecting the ideal itself and promoting acceptance of one’s body as it is, across shapes, sizes and appearances (e.g. “real beauty” campaigns, size-inclusive fashion, unretouched ads — slide images: Nike leggings, L’Oréal “Lesson of Worth”)

À retenir

Both are marketing opportunities: brands can help consumers pursue the ideal or build equity by challenging it altogether.

07Composante 3Gender & gender socialization
  • A person’s biological gender does not totally determine whether they exhibit sex-typed traits (characteristics stereotypically associated with one gender or the other).
  • Gender identity (shaped by sociocultural factors) ≠ sexual identity: more a state of mind as well as body.
  • Society holds expectations (sex-roles) that change over time and differ across cultures → are gender differences innate or culturally shaped? (question asked on the slide).
  • Toward greater gender fluidity: marketers must track how the meaning of gender evolves. Segments: LGBTQ+ consumers (growing segment still facing stereotypes); androgyny (consumers combining masculine and feminine traits). The quest for gender justice remains slow and uneven.

Consumer socialization (Roedder John, 1999)

The process by which young people acquire skills, knowledge and attitudes relevant to their functioning in the marketplace.

Parenting styleDescription
AuthoritarianNo warm relationships, censor media, negative view of advertising
NeglectingDetached from kids, little control over what they do
IndulgentCommunicate and want children to learn about buying

Friends & peers also shape consumer learning, as do television and the web (“the electronic babysitter”, teaching a culture’s values, myths and idealized images).

Gender socialization: children learn gender-specific expectations too. They pick up gender identity by age three (e.g. driving a truck = masculine, cooking and cleaning = feminine). Play rehearses adulthood: toys teach children the expectations society has of them. Slide images: Vileda cleaning set vs Bosch workstation; BBC clip of a toddler.

Reinforcing stereotypesChallenging stereotypes
Depicting men and women in traditional, expected roles; playing it safe (e.g. Axe’s hyper-masculine “Axe Effect” campaigns)Undermining traditional gender roles and their impact on self-esteem (e.g. Always’ “#LikeAGirl”)

Both can work: reinforcing feels safe and familiar; challenging can build strong brand equity, but only when it feels authentic rather than opportunistic.

08CEMSelf-empowerment through brands: self-image congruence

Self-image congruence models

We choose products when their attributes match some aspect of the self.

The match (consumers compare a brand’s symbolic attributes — rugged, sophisticated, rebellious — with their self-image) → psychological connection (stronger match = deeper emotional bond, self-brand connection) → behavioural outcomes (higher congruence → more positive attitudes, preference, purchase intention and loyalty).

  • Consumers perceive customized products more positively because they allow more alignment with their self-image.
  • We become attached to an object to the extent we rely on it to maintain our self-concept; we form relationships with products resembling the bonds we form with people.

3 brand strategies — turning congruence into customer experience

  1. Reaching the different selves (strong symbolic dimension, especially matching the Ideal Self)
  2. Building brand personality (convincing consumers the brand matches who they are)
  3. Increasing self-esteem (directly strengthening how consumers feel about themselves)

1 — Reaching the different selves

Self-imageDefinitionSlide example
Actual self-imageWho I really am right nowIKEA: practical, affordable furniture reflecting who consumers really are day to day, not an aspirational lifestyle
Ideal self-imageWho I would like to be or aspire to becomeNike / Apple: a top running shoe or an Apple Watch Ultra is about the person you want to become
Social self-imageHow I believe others currently see mePatagonia: wearing the brand publicly signals environmental values and love of nature
Ideal social self-imageHow I would like others to see meRolex / Ferrari: project the success, power and prestige you want others to perceive

2 — Brand personality

Brand personality

The set of traits people attribute to a product, as if it were a person. Like our relationships with people, these can change over time. A desirable brand personality is often key to building brand loyalty.

Aaker (1997): 5 dimensionsFacetsSlide brand
SincerityDown-to-earth, honest, wholesome, cheerfulMichel & Augustin
ExcitementDaring, spirited, imaginative, up-to-dateRed Bull
CompetenceReliable, intelligent, successfulMichelin
SophisticationUpper class, charmingChanel
RuggednessTough, outdoorsyQuechua

Going further: brand storytelling (consumers engage by “filling in the blanks”) and archetypes (Jung): advertisers draw on them for a vivid personality (wizards, revered teachers, Mother Nature).

3 — Increasing self-esteem: Dove

Rather than pushing an unattainable ideal, Dove closes the gap by validating the actual self — a direct application of self-esteem theory to brand strategy.

Exemple du cours — Dove

YearInitiative
2004Campaign for Real Beauty: unretouched women of different ages, shapes and tones; the actual self becomes the new ideal
2006–2013Evolution & Real Beauty Sketches: expose how media distort self-image and how harshly we judge our own appearance
2021No Digital Distortion Mark: pledges no retouching; removes “normal” from packaging and marketing
OngoingSelf-Esteem Project: workshops and teaching materials reaching over 90 million young people worldwide
