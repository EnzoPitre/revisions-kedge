Perception, Learning & Memory — Sensory Marketing

Consumer Behavior & Customer Experience Management · Session 2

Idée centrale

The brain processes sensory stimuli along two dimensions — cognitive (mental processing) and affective (emotional response) — before any meaning is assigned. Consumers want more than function: they want an experience, which puts product design, packaging and store atmospherics at the centre of strategy. Memorable experiences are those that are sensory-rich, emotionally charged and personally relevant.

01ProcessusPerception: from sensation to interpretation
TermCourse definition
SensationThe biochemical signals our senses send to the brain. Sensory receptors (eyes, ears, nose, mouth, fingers, skin) capture basic stimuli (light, colour, sound, odour, texture)
PerceptionThe process by which people select, organize and interpret sensations. It studies what we add to the raw sensations to give them meaning

The three stages

Exposure → Attention → Interpretation (stimuli: sights, sounds, smells, tastes, textures → receptors: eyes, ears, nose, mouth, skin).

Exemples du cours (slide 5)

The iconic Orangina bottle (designed by Jean-Claude Beton, 1936) · Jo Malone’s flagship scent bar in London (designed for hands-on fragrance discovery) · the Kit Kat street campaign (2016): a bench designed to look and feel like the chocolate bar.

02Étape 1Exposure & sensory thresholds

Exposure

Occurs when a stimulus comes within range of the sensory receptors. We can concentrate, ignore, or completely miss stimuli.

ThresholdDefinition
Absolute threshold (seuil)The minimum amount of stimulation a person can detect on a given sensory channel
Differential thresholdThe ability of a sensory system to detect changes or differences between two stimuli. The minimum detectable difference = the j.n.d. (just noticeable difference)

Exemple du cours — absolute thresholds

SenseThreshold
VisionA candle flame 30 miles away
HearingA watch ticking 20 feet away
SmellA drop of perfume in a six-room house
TasteA teaspoon of sugar in a gallon of water
TouchA fly’s wing on your cheek, dropped from 1 cm

Slide image next to the differential threshold: the Pepsi logos from 1898 to 2005 (no commentary given).

Thresholds are not fixed

They vary from person to person (biological differences: age, genetics, sensory acuity) and with context and attention. Use: design logos, packaging, scents and sounds that are perceptible without being overwhelming.

The myth of subliminal perception

A stimulus below the absolute threshold is called subliminal (Vicary, 1957: “Hungry? Eat popcorn”). The idea has fascinated the public for 50+ years. No evidence that subliminal perception has any effect on consumer behaviour: a stimulus below the absolute threshold never even reaches the sensory receptors, so it cannot be processed, let alone influence behaviour.

03Étape 2Attention & perceptual selection

In a cluttered media environment we suffer sensory overload: far more information than we can process. Marketers must break through the noise (original content, teaser campaigns, unexpected formats). Slide examples: Michel & Augustin packaging, a Netflix street campaign near the Arc de Triomphe, an Adidas Originals shoebox pavilion.

Attention & perceptual selection

Attention = the extent to which processing activity is focused on a particular stimulus. It is selective: consumers process only a small fraction of the stimuli they are exposed to = perceptual selection.

MechanismDefinitionExample (slide)
Perceptual vigilanceMore likely to notice stimuli related to current needsShopping for a car → suddenly you notice car ads everywhere
Perceptual defensePeople see what they want to see and screen out what they don’tA heavy smoker mentally blocks images of cancer-scarred lungs
AdaptationHow long consumers keep noticing a stimulus; once too familiar, they stop paying attentionSlide image: roadside billboards
04Étape 3Interpretation & semiotics

Interpretation

The meaning we assign to sensory stimuli: the cognitive process by which we organize and make sense of what our senses capture. We do not interpret in a vacuum: we relate stimuli to prior knowledge stored in memory (past experiences, expectations, personality, cultural background). Primarily cognitive, but often triggers affective/emotional responses.

Semiotics

Every marketing message has an object (the product), a sign (what represents it) and an interpretant (the meaning we derive).

Exemple du cours: object = Nike shoe (brand) · sign = the Swoosh · interpretant = hip-hop culture, urban status symbol.

Signs relate to the object in three ways: icon, index, symbol (slide examples: Michelin’s Bibendum = icon · Mr Clean = index · Apple’s apple = symbol).

05CEMSensory marketing

Sensory marketing

A strategic approach that targets consumers’ five senses (sight, hearing, touch, taste, smell) to create an emotional connection with a brand or product; sensory stimuli guide perceptions and behaviours. Aim: offer not only a product but a memorable experience (pleasant store smell, relaxing music, tactile quality → more positive feelings and loyalty). Slide examples: Perrier × Philippe Starck bottle; Perrier “Melt, Tennis” campaign (Ogilvy Paris).

Experiential marketingSensory marketing
The broader paradigm. Purchasing vs consuming: the transaction is not the experience. Holbrook & Hirschman (1982): fantasies, feelings and fun. Cognitive, behavioural and relational dimensions alongside sensory and emotional aspectsOperational: the practical, applied side of the experiential paradigm. Focuses on the five senses: perceptions, sensations and the emotions they trigger → affective, cognitive and behavioural reactions

Krishna’s model (2012)

Sensation (haptics, olfaction, audition, taste, vision) → perception → emotion and cognition ↔ attitude, learning/memory, behaviour.

08ApprentissageThe learning process

Learning

An ongoing process: our knowledge about the world is constantly revised through experience. It ranges from a simple stimulus–response association (a logo) to complex cognitive activities.

FamilyTheories
Behavioral learningClassical conditioning (Pavlov, 1890) · instrumental/operant conditioning (Skinner, 1940)
Cognitive learningObservational learning / modeling

Classical conditioning (Pavlov, 1890)

Definition

A stimulus that elicits a response is paired with another stimulus that initially does not elicit a response on its own.

Before: food (unconditioned stimulus, UCS) → salivation (unconditioned response, UCR); bell = neutral stimulus. During: bell + food → salivation. After: bell (conditioned stimulus, CS) → salivation (conditioned response, CR).

Exemple du cours — marketing application (McDonald’s)

Before: burger → hungry response (UCS → UCR). During: burger + McDonald’s logo → hungry response (pairing). After: the logo alone → hungry response (CS → CR).

  • Repetition strengthens associations → more exposures = more brand awareness.
  • Extinction: without reinforcement, the conditioned response fades over time.
  • Stimulus generalization: stimuli similar to a conditioned stimulus evoke similar conditioned responses (= the halo effect). Strategies: family branding, product line extensions, look-alike packaging (slide images: Yamaha logos, Kinder range, Lion / Tiger bars).

Instrumental / operant conditioning (Skinner, 1940)

Definition

We learn to perform behaviours that produce positive outcomes and to avoid those that yield negative outcomes.

ConceptDefinition
Positive reinforcementA reward that strengthens the response
Negative reinforcementAlso strengthens the response, but a negative outcome is avoided
PunishmentUnpleasant events follow a response: we learn the hard way not to repeat it

À retenir

Reinforcement (positive or negative) makes a behaviour appear; punishment is intended to make an unwanted behaviour disappear.

Applications: frequency marketing (rewards regular purchasers with prizes whose value increases as they spend more; slide: Air France-KLM Flying Blue tiers, Starbucks Rewards) and gamification (gaming principles to motivate and reward consumers as they ascend levels of mastery, turning routine actions into experiences; slide: Nike Run Club, Duolingo).

Cognitive learning: observational learning (modeling)

Definition

Cognitive learning stresses internal mental processes; people are problem solvers who use information from the world, including what they observe others saying and doing. Observational learning = we watch others and note the reinforcements they receive (= vicarious, indirect learning).

StepContent
1. AttentionWe pay more attention to high-status, talented or similar models
2. RetentionThe behaviour must be retained in memory (rehearse/encode)
3. Motor reproductionRequires the skills, knowledge or resources to perform it (practice/feedback)
4. MotivationReproducing the behaviour requires intrinsic or external motivation (reward/reinforce)

Marketing application: show what happens to desirable models who use — or don’t use — the product; consumers often imitate later (celebrity endorsement). Social attractiveness matters more than the celebrity’s image alone; its specific drivers are physical appearance, expertise, similarity.

09MémoireMemory processes and systems

Memory

The process of acquiring information and storing it over time so it is available when needed.

StageContent
1. EncodingInformation is acquired and converted into a storable format (sensory or meaning-based)
2. StorageMaintaining the encoded information over time: short-term memory (quick access) or long-term memory (sustained retention)
3. RetrievalStored information brought back to conscious awareness: recall (without cues) or recognition (identifying from a list or group of options)
Memory systemRoleCapacityDuration
Sensory memoryTemporary storage of information received from the sensesLow< 1 second (vision); a few seconds (smell, hearing)
Short-term memoryBrief storage of information currently being usedLimited< 20 seconds
Long-term memoryRelatively permanent storageUnlimitedLong or permanent

Transfers

  • Attention: information that passes through an attentional gate goes from sensory to short-term memory.
  • Elaboration: information subjected to elaborative rehearsal or deep processing goes to long-term memory.

Flashbulb memory: a vivid, detailed memory of a highly emotional or significant event; these memories appear to be more accurate and longer lasting (slide images: 9/11, the pandemic).

10ApplicationProducts as memory makers

Products are powerful memory makers connecting consumers to their personal histories and emotions, notably through nostalgia.

Nostalgia & retro brands

Nostalgia = “the bittersweet emotion that arises when we view the past with both sadness and longing”. Retro brands = updated versions of products or brand identities from a prior historical period, blending the familiar aesthetics or values of the past with the innovation and relevance of the present. Slide examples: Bonne Maman jam, Fiat 500.

À retenir

By positioning products as vessels of memory, brands turn everyday consumption into a meaningful experience: consumers relive moments, reconnect with their identity and share memories across generations.
