Question centrale

How can an organization — whose habits, rules and success criteria were built around economic performance — be transformed so that it can design and develop more sustainable innovations?

01ConceptOrganizational culture

Definitions in the course

  • Schein & Schein (2017): culture emerges from accumulated and shared learnings developed to solve problems of adaptation to the environment and internal integration. They define the appropriate way to perceive, think, feel and act.
  • Chatman & O’Reilly (2016) / Delobbe & de Boer (2023): the set of norms that guides members’ attitudes and behaviours; it enables and channels social regulation and supports socialisation (especially of new arrivals).
Schein’s 3 componentsContent
1. Visible artifacts and behavioursPractices, procedures, meetings, decisions, indicators, ways of operating
2. Espoused valuesWhat the company states is important: commitment, responsibility, innovation, sustainability
3. Fundamental assumptionsImplicit beliefs, e.g. “The customer always chooses the cheapest option”; “Only financial results matter”

Exemple du cours — Toyota

“Continuous improvement and respect for people in everything we do.” The Toyota Way: learning organisation valuing mutual trust and long-term prosperity. Five principles: Respect, Challenge, Teamwork, Continuous improvement, Genchi Genbutsu (“go to the source”: verify the facts yourself to make a good decision).

Exemple du cours — Decathlon

“Focus on what you enjoy doing; you’ve got the right to try, to make mistakes and to try again.” Four values: Vitality (positive, full of energy), Responsibility (take charge of one’s life), Authenticity (true to oneself and others), Generosity (wholeheartedly, considerate of others). In a 2022 interview (slide 8, in French), Michel Leclercq says the two most important are Generous and Responsible, the most original in the capitalist system — “ce ‘et’ est important”.

02DéfinitionCulture focused on sustainability

Principle

Sustainability becomes a feature of culture when it influences values, beliefs, norms and day-to-day decisions, not merely the company’s stated commitments or environmental projects.

Two definitions

  • Linnenluecke & Griffiths (2010): a culture in which shared values and beliefs give sustainability a place within the organisation’s objectives and decisions.
  • Assoratgoon & Kantabutra (2023): a set of fundamental assumptions, values and shared beliefs relating to the resolution of sustainability issues, which shape organisational behaviour through decisions and practices.
Schein levelSustainability version (course adaptation)
Fundamental assumptionsThe organisation regards itself as interdependent with society and the environment
Values and normsResponsibility, cooperation and consideration of long-term impacts are legitimate decision criteria
Artifacts and behavioursEco-design, responsible procurement criteria, training, cooperation mechanisms, environmental and social indicators

Piège : « green culture » ≠ sustainability culture

A green organizational culture focuses only on the environment. Piwowar-Sulej (2020) offers a framework for a pro-environmental culture, distinct from the dimensions of sustainable development: environmental (preserve ecosystems and resources, limit pollution), social (needs, rights and well-being of people and stakeholders), economic (viability and long-term value creation).

Four dimensions of a pro-sustainability culture (slide 13): 1. Politics & practices (importance given to the environment) · 2. Responsibility of sustainable resources (allocation of responsibilities, externalities) · 3. Communication & HR (measures for skills and awareness) · 4. Attitudes & behaviours (ownership in daily roles).

03AttentionGreenwashing

Greenwashing (eco-whitewashing): communication practices presenting a misleading environmental image of a company, product or service. It is not always an explicit lie: it may come from a “sincere” but poorly implemented policy. The DGCCRF distinguishes unfounded, misleading or disproportionate claims; a claim must be clear, verifiable and justified, and must not conceal significant life-cycle impacts.

MechanismExample (slide 14)
Vague claim“Eco-friendly” without saying how or giving evidence
Selective highlightsPromoting a small green initiative as if it were the bulk of the business
Overestimated benefitsPresenting a limited emissions reduction as elimination of climate impact
Promise not substantiatedFuture carbon-neutrality goal without means and assumptions
Hidden impact transferHighlighting recyclable packaging while ignoring impacts at other life-cycle stages

Exemples du cours

  • Volkswagen “clean diesel”: low-emission claims in the US while some vehicles had software to manipulate emission tests. FTC legal action March 2016; settlements June 2016 (buy-backs, compensation, funds to reduce pollution and support clean automotive technologies).
  • KLM: sued by Fossielvrij over adverts, notably “Fly Responsibly”. On 20 March 2024 the Amsterdam court found several communications misleading and unlawful (too general claims; overly optimistic portrayal of alternative fuels and reforestation). KLM may still publicise initiatives if honest and concrete.
04Cas + listeIKEA and the drivers of transformation

Exemple du cours — IKEA (Elg & Welinder, 2022)

IKEA develops circular business models (collection and resale of furniture, repairs, lifespan extension), adapting a business historically built on design, manufacture and sale of new furniture. → Contradictions between management strategy, product development and commercial practices: sustainable innovation requires transforming the whole value creation system. Transition underway: from selling new furniture to a system emphasising lifespan, repair, reuse and material recovery.

Examples: sustainable-focused design (raw materials, manufacturing for recycling) · transformed supplier relations (long-term agreements) · training in-store sales staff for the recycling business · logistics decarbonisation · 2018: shared sustainability strategy across the franchise network · commitment to become “people and planet positive” by 2030.

Drivers for more sustainable innovations (slide 18)

  1. Collectively redefine the criteria for sustainable innovation
  2. Embody transformation through exemplary leadership
  3. Align management and recognition systems
  4. Develop skills and supporting professional identities
  5. Encourage experimentation and challenging established routines
  6. Develop a culture of cooperation and open innovation
  7. Formalise long-term commitments with key partners, suppliers and stakeholders
  8. Embed the transformation in daily routines and collective learning

Titre incohérent

The slide title says “5 drivers” but lists 8. Learn the 8 listed.

05Article obligatoireAssoratgoon & Kantabutra (2023): SOC model

Aim: find cutting-edge knowledge on sustainability organizational culture (SOC) through an integrated systematic literature review (Scopus, 1995-2021, 935 documents, bibliometrics + grounded theory).

  • Gap: research focuses on productivity culture and on artifacts/values; the level of basic assumptions (normative grounding) is the least studied. Business-case (win-win) logic dominates the field; the normative logic is less influential.
  • Four growth phases: pre-growth 1995-2001 (12 docs) · emergent 2002-2008 (80) · steady growth 2009-2016 (357) · fast growth 2017-2021 (486).
  • Four schools of thought: (1) organizational culture, leadership and change; (2) corporate sustainability strategy and management; (3) technological innovation in sustainable manufacturing and supply chain; (4) frameworks and methods.
  • Most influential scholars: Griffiths, Baumgartner, Gunasekaran, Jabbour, Lozano, Kantabutra. Most influential documents: Siebenhüner & Arnold (2007), Baumgartner (2009).
Lozano — cultural transformationKantabutra — SOC framework
FocusProcess: how to transform a culture toward sustainability, starting at the assumption levelContent: components of a sustainability culture (values and beliefs level)
ElementsContext · causal conditions (internal, connecting, external drivers) · enablers (hard and soft issues) and barriers · 5 transformation strategies (orchestrated and iterative change; simultaneous and holistic change; congruence across organisational levels; congruence across cultural levels; organizational learning) · collaboration vs individualistic culture · outputs (new insights, long-term behavioural change) and outcomes (sustainability innovations, reputation, resilience, industry transformation)Sustainability vision and sustainability values (perseverance, social and environmental responsibility, innovation, generosity, ethics/integrity) → sustainable leadership practices and sustainability practices (perseverance, resilience development, moderation, geosocial development, knowledge sharing) → corporate sustainability

Managerial implications: align vision, values, leadership practices and organisational practices; be aware of enabling/inhibiting conditions; understand benefits and challenges of collaboration; use transformation strategies.

Leadership that fosters a culture of innovation (slide 22)

  • Everyone feels responsible for innovation; innovation is present throughout the organisation.
  • Management is receptive to ideas from all employees; all managers encourage innovation.
  • A budget is allocated to innovation in line with the vision.
  • Long-term process where “Experiment & Learn” go hand in hand.

Exemple du cours — Interface

Ray Anderson, founder, in 1994 set the goal of eliminating negative environmental impacts: strategic plan “Zero Mission”. Challenged the industrial model, assembled a team (scientists, entrepreneurs, environmental specialists), linked environmental strategy with staff training and financial incentives. Net-Works: recycling used fishing nets into carpet = social innovation (new income for fishermen) + circular economy.

Exemple du cours — Renault Refactory

Launched in 2020 by J-D. Senard (Chairman) and Luca de Meo (CEO): the Flins plant, historically making new cars, turns to circular economy activities; a training campus for circular economy careers and an open innovation scheme (start-ups, experts, industrial partners).

Inspirational leadership — Goffee & Jones

Source of inspirationContent
1. Situational awarenessUnderstand complex situations, adapt, manage trade-offs, emotions and tensions; take time to observe
2. Strong empathyLoyalty, positivity, emotional intelligence, respect, inclusive diversity, team cohesion; helps teams grow and find solutions
3. Recognise strengths and weaknessesAuthenticity, humility
4. Dare to be differentUnderstand others, align with company values, open to innovation and experimentation, visuals and metaphors

Inspiration does not rely only on an ambitious vision: it depends on the leader’s authenticity, understanding of people and ability to build trust.

07Article obligatoireRehman & Zeb (2023): authentic leadership and green creativity
  • Authentic leadership (slide): a leader aware of strengths and limits, transparent in relationships, consistent with values, open to different points of view. In the article (Walumbwa et al. 2008): multifaceted — internal moral perspective, relational transparency, balanced processing, leader self-awareness.
  • Green creativity: employees’ potential and roles to generate and initiate green innovative ideas.
  • Method: questionnaire (5-point Likert) in technical training centres for renewable energy projects, Rawalpindi/Islamabad (Pakistan); 450 forms sent, 367 complete (81.56%); convenience sampling; PLS-SEM, 5,000 bootstrap subsamples. Theories: social identity and social exchange.
HypothesisResult
H1 Authentic leadership → green creativity (β = 0.126)Accepted
H2 Authentic leadership → environment of trust and safety (β = 0.111)Rejected (p = 0.146)
H3 Authentic leadership → self-efficacy (β = 0.211)Accepted
H4 Trust and safety → green creativity (β = 0.180)Accepted
H5 Self-efficacy → green creativity (β = 0.330)Accepted
H6 Trust/safety mediates leadership → creativity (β = 0.020, p = 0.247)Rejected
H7 Self-efficacy mediates leadership → creativity (β = 0.070, p = 0.002)Accepted (partial mediation)

Course summary of findings: (1) authentic leadership is positively associated with green creativity; (2) self-efficacy mediates; (3) trust and safety are associated with creativity but their mediating role is not confirmed. Limits: only technical training centres in two regions (generalisability), cross-sectional design.

Incohérences à connaître

  • H2: the text gives t = 0.456, Table 5 gives 1.456 (consistent with p = 0.146).
  • The article says R² explains only 19.1% of green creativity variance (1.2% for trust and safety, 4.5% for self-efficacy): a modest explanatory power.
  • The slide speaks of “trust and job security”; the article’s construct is “environment of trust and safety”.
