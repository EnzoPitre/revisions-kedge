Idée centrale

Evaluating a sustainable innovation is not only showing that it yields environmental or social benefits. It also means measuring its impacts, identifying unintended / adverse side effects, and determining whether it genuinely contributes to transforming the system.

Social and environmental impact

The totality of economic, social, environmental and cultural consequences that an activity or project generates for a community or society — positive or negative (e.g. job creation vs social exclusion or environmental damage).

Course summary of social impact: aggregate of positive or negative, intended or unintended, lasting changes brought about by implemented activities and directly attributable to them; environmental, economic or social in nature.

  • Concerns external stakeholders (beneficiaries, users, direct/indirect clients) and internal ones (employees, volunteers), society and the environment.
  • Concept introduced in the 1970s through academic research at Yale University on investors’ ethical responsibilities.
  • Understood through an impact value chain: each link feeds the next; impact depends on context, actors, time horizons, and the ability to anticipate underserved needs through prevention, remediation or compensation.

03PourquoiWhy evaluate?

Three questions to measure impact effectively: What? (which outcomes, how important for stakeholders) · Who? (which populations, how underserved before) · How much? (how many people, depth/scale of change, how long it lasts). → evaluates breadth, depth and durability.

Purpose: systematic risk prevention and active remediation of harms (social, environmental, human rights, governance) — with traceability and transparency.

Evaluate to…Content
DemonstrateTangible evidence (monitor strategic execution) · verify the innovation delivers its sustainability promise · show value to all stakeholders → indicators tailored to each stakeholder group
ActCorrect or stabilise (organizational learning) · see which value-chain stages generate the most sustainability · systemic life-cycle approach · legitimise new behaviours (consumer “education”)
CommunicateKnow how to let others know · tailor messages by stakeholder · protect credibility (avoid greenwashing) → be clear, accessible and transparent about impacts and methods

Examples shown: Patagonia Worn Wear (repairs with guides and tutorial videos); Renault Flins Refactory.

04MéthodeHow to measure: qualitative approach

Measuring impact = determining what changes an innovation produces in the medium/long term, for whom, at what scale, and to what extent these changes can be attributed to it. Two approaches: qualitative and quantitative / monetized (SROI).

Qualitative approach (slide 14)

Identify the 5 dimensions of impact; for each, rate its level/significance from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong).

Cas du cours (fil rouge)

A non-profit provides low-cost electric cars + driving training to long-term-illness patients who are job seekers in underserved/isolated areas, so they can attend medical appointments. 100 patients, 1 year, €100,000.

05MéthodeSROI: the 6 steps

SROI (Social Return on Investment)

An accounting/evaluative method that measures and assigns monetary value to the social, environmental and economic value created by a project or investment, complementing traditional financial metrics.

  1. Establish scope and identify key stakeholders (internal and external). Key question: how do we engage those who are overlooked or rarely consulted?
  2. Map outcomes (clarify the change): impact map co-created with stakeholders, inputs → activities → outcomes; based on a theory of change / logic model.
  3. Evidence outcomes and give them value: indicators, data collection protocol (who, what, when), financial proxies.
  4. Establish impact: deadweight, attribution, displacement, drop-off.
  5. Calculate the SROI: future projections → NPV → ratio → sensitivity analysis → (optional) payback period.
  6. Report, use and embed.

Step 4 — the deductions

ConceptMeaningSlide example
DeadweightPart of the outcome that would have happened anyway, without the intervention; needs a baseline benchmarkNatural exit rate from unemployment benefits for long-term job seekers in the same region (vs a vocational programme)
AttributionShare of the outcome due to others’ contributions (other organisations/interventions)Solar lamps: change not due to falling kerosene prices
DisplacementHow much the outcome simply displaced other positive outcomes or shifted a problem elsewhereStreet lighting cuts crime in one area, adjacent precincts see crime rise; ex-offender jobs that would have been filled by other job seekers
Drop-offDecline of the outcome’s value over time (applied to later years)20% per year in the case

Net impact, year 1

\[ \text{Gross value} = \text{Quantity} \times \text{Proxy} \]

\[ \text{Year 1 net} = \text{Gross value} \times (1-\text{deadweight}) \times (1-\text{attribution}) \times (1-\text{displacement}) \]

Later years: apply the drop-off each year (year 2 = year 1 × (1 − drop-off)).

Present value, NPV, SROI ratio

\[ PV = \sum_{t} \frac{\text{net impact}_t}{(1+r)^t} \qquad NPV = PV - \text{Investment} \qquad SROI = \frac{PV}{\text{Investment}} \]

Here \(r\) = discount rate (3.5% in the course case). The ratio is read as “€X of social value for every €1 invested”.

Check, e.g. health: 60,000 × 0.80 × 0.80 = 38,400. Year-1 total = 96,720. Year 2 (drop-off 20% on the three 2-year outcomes, facilities stop after 1 year) = 30,720 + 24,480 + 14,400 = 69,600. Total before discounting 166,320.

Step 5 — result (slide 24)

  • Year 1: 96,720 / 1.035 = 93,449; Year 2: 69,600 / 1.035² ≈ 64,968 (slide) → PV = 158,417.
  • Investment 100,000 → NPV = 58,417; SROI = 1.58 : 1.
  • Interpretation: in this pedagogical scenario every €1 invested generates about €1.58 of discounted social value.

Petit écart de calcul

Recomputing 69,600 / 1.035² gives 64,972, not 64,968, so PV ≈ 158,422 and NPV ≈ 58,422. The ratio is still 1.58. Use the slide’s figures for the course, but expect a rounding difference if you recompute.

Step 5 sub-steps (slide 23): (1) future projections with drop-off; (2) NPV: discount to present value; (3) ratio = total discounted PV ÷ initial investment; (4) sensitivity analysis: test how the ratio moves when key assumptions change; (5) optional payback period: time for cumulative discounted social benefits to match the investment.

07Article (lecture non obligatoire)Hutchinson et al. (2020): modified vehicles

Question: SROI of vehicle modifications for people with disability (Australia). No prior financial evaluation existed. Primary data: 23 interviews (8 consumers + 15 other stakeholders). Framework: Nicholls et al. (2012) six stages.

Specific features of the studyWhy
Five scenarios (low to high cost: scenario 1 AUD 3,652.29 → scenario 5 AUD 144,999.40)It evaluates a type of intervention, not one programme with known costs
Weightings (share of drivers expected to experience each outcome)The number of users was unknown
Co-investment model (funder + consumer)Consumers bear much of the cost (87% in scenario 1)
  • 13 outcomes (11 consumer-related, plus reduced burden on caregivers and increased community awareness); benefit period 10 years (conservative); discount 2.5% per year for years 2-10 (inflation).
  • Only deadweight and attribution were estimated (no displacement, no drop-off since the vehicle remains).

Sensitivity analysis: proxies, deadweight and attribution reduced by 50%; top-5 outcomes removed in turn; benefit period cut from 10 to 5 years (co-investment SROI of scenario 5 falls to $1.47). Limits of SROI (introduction): time and resource intensive · soft outcomes hard to quantify · ratios are context-specific, not generalisable and cannot be compared. Strengths: measures outcomes other methods miss; simple ratio easy to understand.

  • Why does the SROI subtract deadweight and attribution — what is the difference?

  • Why did Hutchinson et al. use five scenarios and weightings?
