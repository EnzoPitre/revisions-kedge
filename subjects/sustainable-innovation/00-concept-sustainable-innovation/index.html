Concept of Sustainable Innovation (Circular, Frugal, Social)

Sustainable Innovation · Session 1

16 minFicheDefinition of sustainable innovation, classic innovation (Schumpeter, incremental vs radical, value creation/capture), and the three forms: circular, frugal and social innovation.

Sommaire

Idée centrale

Sustainable innovation does not replace the general logic of innovation: it expands it and adds complexity (new types of value, externalities, a wider value system, a wider scope of analysis).

01DéfinitionSustainable innovation

General definition (course)

The intentional introduction of new or significantly improved products, services, processes, business models or organizational practices, aimed at simultaneously creating economic, environmental and social value within a long-term perspective.

Formula of the slide

Sustainable innovation = Innovation + economic value + environmental value + social value, over the long term, for a variety of internal and external stakeholders.

4 essential elements

  1. There must be genuine innovation.
  2. It is not just about technology.
  3. It is collaborative and open innovation.
  4. It aims to improve multiple dimensions of the organization’s performance.

Context: the 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) of the 2030 Agenda, which balance the economic, social and environmental dimensions (Suchek et al., 2021).

02BaseInnovation: the classic approach
TermCourse definition
Idea – discoveryFinding out what was unknown but pre-existing
InventionRelies on principles, ideas, theories; often enclosed in R&D labs; can stay disconnected from the market and remain an idea forever
InnovationEvery phase from prototype development to market launch. Schumpeter: invention = realization of a new resource for the company; innovation = integration of this resource in a product/service offered to the market

Main idea: not all ideas lead to innovation (a new product/service delivered to the market). There is no consensus on a single definition.

Schumpeter (1912, The Theory of Economic Development) — 5 sources of innovation

  1. Production of a new merchandise
  2. A new manufacturing method of an old merchandise
  3. Exploitation of a new raw material source
  4. Conquest of a new market
  5. Reorganization of a production system

The entrepreneur’s role = “transforming the production routine, by exploiting an invention or an original technical possibility”.

  • N. Alter (2000): any activity, product or service that questions, more or less deeply, the rules, frameworks and norms (institutional, professional, regulatory…) of a field.
  • Van de Ven (1986): a new idea, possibly a recombination of old ideas, a scheme that challenges the present order, or a formula perceived as new by those involved.
PerspectiveInnovation is…
FirmWhat creates distinctive value to consolidate a competitive position or create a new market
User / customerA shift in behaviour and associated values
Other stakeholdersOnly possible if they believe in it and support the firm and the user (suppliers, distribution, financiers, prescribers)

Where does innovation lie? Manufacturing methods · development process · cooperation between departments (managerial innovation) · exploitation of new components · new ideas (invention). Innovation is systemic: it requires innovating in several domains.

03DistinctionIncremental vs radical innovation
IncrementalRadical
WhatImproves performance of an existing product/serviceSimultaneously and completely alters the technological, societal and market reference points; a break with the existing standard
UsageNew features do not fundamentally alter purchase/usage patternsUsage conditions change; must be adopted by customers → higher risk
ImpactDoes not disrupt market or organizationResult of technological breakthroughs (technical mastery + deep market understanding linked)
OriginContinuous improvement process; keeps the firm ahead of imitatorsIncreasingly from new societal and civic expectations (responsible, social, sustainable innovation)

Why distinguish them? Strategic: firms build new markets with radical innovation and sustain them against imitators with incremental innovation; radical innovation shows dynamism and opens new growth. Managerial: proves the firm can reinvent itself and integrate new ideas and external skills; motivates teams.

04ConceptValue creation vs value capture
  1. An innovation creates value when it delivers superior benefits.
  2. Distinguish value creation from value capture.

For whom? Not only the end consumer: B2C (target customers, values delivered), external B2B (key partners in the value chain), internal B2B (employees).

LogicValue capture =
B2CPerceived difference between “risks/costs of novelty” and “usage benefits”
External B2BDifference between “investment & operating costs” and captured long-term values
Internal B2BDifference between “risks & effort invested” and captured internal values

Competitive advantage: does the innovation lead to one (cost strategy or upward differentiation)? How to build a long-term one — sources difficult to imitate and difficult to substitute?

07Forme 1Circular innovation

Circular economy (course)

An economic concept based on the regeneration and restoration of raw materials at each stage of the manufacturing and use cycle of a product. Objective: produce goods and services while strongly limiting consumption and waste of raw materials and non-renewable energy.

  • Eco-innovation: innovate from environmental constraints by carrying out an eco-balance of an existing product (example: Tetra Pak renewable carton).
  • Cradle to cradle (C2C): products designed either to enrich the ecosystem after use or to become 100% reusable technical nutrients; coined by Braungart and McDonough in 2002; basis of a certification label; “upcycled” products (raw materials preserved through infinite life cycles). Example: Desso (Tarkett).
  • Natural capital — Ellen MacArthur Foundation, 3 principles: 1. preserve and develop natural capital (control finite stocks, balance renewable flows); 2. optimize resource use (circulate products, components, materials at their best performance in the biological and technical cycle); 3. create the conditions of a virtuous system by identifying and eliminating negative externalities.

Suchek et al. (2021) — systematic review

  • Linear model “take-make-dispose” reaching its limits; waste estimated at 2.59 billion t by 2030 and 3.40 billion t by 2050.
  • Method: Web of Science, “innovation*” and “circular econom*”; 109 articles → 83 after screening; bibliographic coupling (VOSviewer) → 7 clusters. First article in 2016; strong rise from 2018.
  • CE = systemic change (companies, industries, economies), radical shifts in values, norms, behaviour. Barriers: cultural (consumers’ lack of interest, hesitant company cultures) are the most significant (Kirchherr et al.).
  • Prieto-Sandoval: 8 eco-innovation types: business model, network, organizational structure, process, product, service, market, client involvement.
  • Bocken et al.: slowing resource cycles (longer life, repair, remanufacturing), closing them (recycling), reducing resource flow (efficiency). Circular business models carry higher risk than linear ones (Linder & Williander).

Conclusions of the review

  • Business model innovation is a fundamental input; main changes: cleaner production, pollution control, waste management, product-service logic, reverse logistics.
  • Radical innovations are necessary: incremental ones are susceptible to rebound effects.
  • Incumbents can influence the ecosystem but are less flexible than start-ups for radical innovation.
  • CE transition requires re-signifying waste, changing the business model, senior management support, measuring sustainability performance, understanding clients, collaborating with stakeholders (Rattalino’s 5 practices).
  • Limits: one database; mostly European and fashion/manufacturing studies.
08Forme 2Frugal innovation

Course description

Frugal innovation, or “jugaad” (Hindi: resourcefulness): ingenious solutions in difficult conditions; the solution is purified to the maximum, without superfluity; its simplicity makes it affordable where sophisticated Western solutions are not; aimed at “Bottom of the Pyramid” markets (Prahalad, 2004). Formalised by Radjou, Prabhu and Ahuja. Principle: “Do more with less”; “a matter of human ingenuity” (Radjou, 2015). In developed countries it is a source of inspiration to create value.

Exemples du cours

  • Dacia, the €5,000 car (2004) · Mitticool, refrigerator without electricity (2011) · Nickel, alternative banking (2012).
  • Chotukool (Godrej & Boyce, India): about a third of all food spoils and ~80% of households have no refrigerator.
  • Indian Mangalaayan Mars satellite (2013): developed 3 times faster and at 1/10th of the price of competitors.
  • Low-cost transport as a frugal disruptive offer.

Weyrauch & Herstatt (2016): three defining criteria

An innovation is frugal only if it meets ALL THREE at the same time

  1. Substantial cost reduction — always from the customer perspective (lower purchase price or lower total cost of ownership; at least one). The authors propose at least one third cheaper than comparable solutions.
  2. Concentration on core functionalities — focus on the functions with highest customer benefit; strip the rest.
  3. Optimised performance level — the level actually needed for the purpose and local conditions: neither over-engineered nor too low (sometimes higher, e.g. Indian car horns).
  • Method: literature review (62 articles, 34 usable) + interviews (45 managers/researchers, 34 analysed). Nine attribute categories grouped into cost reduction, core functionality, performance level.
  • Interview agreement (n = 34): functional and focused on essentials 100% · lower initial cost 94% · lower total cost of ownership 79% · minimising material/financial resources 74% · user-friendly 71% · robust 71% · high value and quality 68% · scalable 50% · sustainable 32%.
  • Scalable and sustainable are NOT defining criteria: possible additional characteristics, not required (sustainability is often not the primary focus).
  • Criteria work in emerging and developed markets.

09Forme 3Social innovation

Definitions

  • Higher Council of the Social and Solidarity Economy: new responses to new or poorly met social needs, under current market and social-policy conditions, with the participation and cooperation of relevant actors including users; concerns products/services and also modes of organization and distribution.
  • Stanford Center for Social Innovation (2016): process of developing and deploying effective solutions to challenging and often systemic social and environmental issues; not the privilege of any organizational form; requires collaboration across government, business and non-profit.
  • Quebec Declaration (RQIS, 2011): new ideas, strategies, interventions; new services, products, laws; new types of organizations that meet specific social needs more effectively and sustainably; solutions championed within institutions, organizations or communities; measurable results for everyone, not just select individuals; transformative and systemic scope; inherently creative, breaks with what came before.

Four dynamics (course): entrepreneurial (new players, NGOs, social entrepreneurship) · managerial (fair pay and working conditions, autonomy, inclusion) · citizen (users involved in design) · public authorities’ support (policies, funding, “Third Places”, see session 6). Examples: Malongo, Max Havelaar; co-housing and dementia villages.

Quebec Declaration on Social Innovation

  • “Social” in both processes and results: meets social needs and forges new relations between people/groups who might not collaborate; born in a spirit of openness (precursor of open innovation).
  • Main pitfalls to implementing innovation are rarely technological: they are organizational, social or cultural (governance, democratic processes, work organisation, leadership).
  • Issues named: poverty, inequality, isolation of immigrants, marginalisation, illiteracy, school dropouts, violence, disease, decline of rural communities.

The 12 key factors (RQIS)

  1. Triggered by three things: an unresolved social issue, a conducive context (crisis, policy), willingness of stakeholders to work together.
  2. Collaborative work of several stakeholders, often ones who wouldn’t normally collaborate.
  3. Combines experiential with scientific/technical knowledge and the cultural context → joint production of new knowledge.
  4. Transformative scope, aims at systemic change.
  5. Partners are daring and able to cope with the unknown and inherent risk.
  6. Three forms of leadership: individual (initiators), organizational (support of organizations), collective (community).
  7. Long-term commitment of sponsors is crucial.
  8. Time is fundamental (design, trust, evaluation, dissemination, replication, appropriation).
  9. Needs impact to be recognised and eventually institutionalised.
  10. Stakeholders must adjust to various contexts and a shifting environment.
  11. Transfer, appropriation and sustainability are essential; must be espoused by the target group and meet pre-established goals.
  12. Trust between stakeholders, expressed through shared governance, is “the project’s glue”.

Why support it: more effective, just and sustainable solutions to complex problems · benefits all of society · enhances communities’ ability to act · develops open-innovation expertise.

10 recommendations (summary): reinforce the Quebec system; fund the RQIS (backed by Université du Québec and MDEIE); maintain/expand the community of interest; permanent independent advisory board; reports on contributions; showcase Quebec innovations; nurture a culture of social innovation; foster experimentation; supportive environment (financing, training, research, transfer, assessment); assign the SQRI’s last structuring project to social innovation.
