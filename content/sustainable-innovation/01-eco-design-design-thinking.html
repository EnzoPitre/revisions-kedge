Message clé de la séance

An innovation is not sustainable simply because the product is better designed. It becomes sustainable when the value proposition, the value chain and the business model are consistent with the sustainability objective.

Key question: how can we move from a commitment to sustainability to a concrete, viable innovation that is consistent with the entire value chain?

01DéfinitionEco-design

Eco-design (sustainable design) — course definition

An approach that aims, right from the initial phases of the innovation process (imagination, creativity, problem framing), to satisfy a need or function while reducing environmental impacts, creating social value, and ensuring economic viability across the entire life cycle of the new product or service and for all its stakeholders.

It leads us to rethink not only the product or service, but also its usage, associated services, value chain and business model.

Inspired by: Steux & Aggeri (2021) ; Prendeville et al. (2017) ; Spangenberg et al. (2010).

4 key points

  1. Consider the entire life cycle.
  2. Move from a purely environmental focus to sustainability.
  3. Move from the product to a product-service offering.
  4. Move from the product (or service) to a systemic approach that includes internal and external factors.

Ecodesign — definition in Prendeville et al. (2017)

A collaborative, proactive and systematic design and management process that considers the full life-cycle environmental impacts of packaging, products, processes, services, organisations and systems. It is a life-cycle management approach that avoids uncoordinated product planning: e.g. eliminating a toxic substance should not lead to higher energy consumption.

02ModèleDesign thinking — standard approach

Design Thinking (course definition)

A discipline that uses the sensitivity, tools and methods of designers to enable interdisciplinary teams to innovate by matching desirability (user expectations), technological feasibility and economic viability.

Principles:

  • Structured method in 5 key steps.
  • Based on understanding the customer experience and its uses to imagine an innovative solution.
  • Combines desirability, feasibility and viability.
  • Continuous experiential approach: develop hypotheses → prototype and experiment (real object, real or virtual model, diagram, drawing…).
  • Uses personas.
  • Used by a team as open and diversified as possible (internal services, customers, partners…).
StepWhat you doGuiding question (slide)
1. EmpathizeLearn about the audience by observation and interviewWho is my user? What matters to this person?
2. DefineCreate a point of view based on user needs and insightsWhat are their needs?
3. IdeateBrainstorm, as many creative solutions as possible (wild ideas encouraged)—
4. PrototypeBuild a representation of one or more ideas to show othersHow can I show my idea? A prototype is just a rough draft!
5. TestShare the prototype with the original user for feedbackWhat worked? What didn’t?

The 3 pillars

Ideas, hypotheses, prototypes and experiments are evaluated and retained at all times against three pillars: Desirability (consumer expectations) · Feasibility (functional and achievable in the foreseeable future) · Viability / Sustainability (fits into a sustainable business model).

Incohérence dans le cours

The slide lists the third pillar as “Sustainability” but the Venn diagram and the definition say “Viability (business / economic)”. Both are shown in the slides; the definition and Brown/Baldassarre use viability. Check with the professor which word is expected.

Tools: persona and prototypes

  • Persona: an imaginary character representing a target group or segment. Usually has a first name and social and psychological characteristics. Several personas can be used in one project; it can be shown as a storyboard of use.
  • Prototypes, models, objects in progress: visualize and make uses concrete · experiment, test, evaluate · tell “a story” (shared vocabulary) · foster participation of all.

Exemple du cours — Persona

Mrs Isabelle, 78, lives alone, enjoys reading and the theatre, degrading health. Expectation: to have friends. Constraint: lives in an isolated village.

Exemple du cours — Accor

  • DT approaches are based on trust between participants.
  • They require recognising the competence of teams.
  • They are not compatible with the top-down managerial culture that often prevails.
  • They are not solely focused on the end user, and not solely inspired by the engineers’ culture of finding a solution to the question posed.
  • AccorHotels set up Labs in certain hotels to devise solutions with DT.

04ArticleResponsible design thinking (Baldassarre et al., 2024)

New assumption proposed

Design thinking is an approach that supports organisations in collaboratively addressing the challenges of sustainable development, by considering desirability, feasibility and viability within responsible innovation processes and outcomes that are ethically acceptable for society and the environment.

Responsibility becomes the overarching criterion: what is ethically acceptable for society and the environment.

Phase (Double Diamond)DT practiceResponsible innovation dimensionOutcome
DiscoveryFraming → reflective framingReflexivityResponsible policies
DefinitionEnvisioning → anticipatory envisioningAnticipationResponsible project ideas
DevelopmentCo-creating → inclusive co-creatingInclusionResponsible coalitions of organisations
DeliveryPrototyping → responsive prototypingResponsivenessResponsible products and services
  • Responsible innovation = experimental and collaborative approach of adaptive learning to solve wicked problems, with an ethical focus on economic, social and environmental impacts.
  • Reflexivity: dialogue to go beyond individual perspectives. Anticipation: think systemically, foresee plausible and desirable outcomes. Inclusion: involve a broader range of stakeholders. Responsiveness: adjust shape and direction as knowledge emerges.
  • Responsible prototyping is “a long marathon”, not a short sprint.

Exemple de l’article — Fairphone vs. orthodox DT

Apple and Samsung are used as cases of balancing desirability, feasibility, viability while ignoring environmental and social impacts. Fairphone treats human rights and resource depletion as cornerstones: fair value chain, upgradable and durable design, brand that nudges consumers to reconsider what they need.

05ArticleEcodesign dilemmas (Prendeville et al., 2017)

Ecodesign dilemma

Scenarios that either pose upfront challenges to the decision-maker, or later lead to unexpected or contradictory outcomes of ecodesign decision-making. Previously called “trade-offs”.

Study: single-firm action-research case study of Orangebox (UK office furniture, B2B), over nine years, four new product development (NPD) cases (chairs). Conclusion: the firm’s learning from dilemmas led it towards business model innovation (pilot of remanufacturing via a leasing contract, 2014).

Type of dilemmaDefinitionCase example
TensionsBilateral tensions between two ecodesign strategiesDisassembly vs durability
HierarchiesSynergies and preclusions between two or more strategies where a single dominant strategy (or reinforcing synergies) precludes othersDurability and recyclability work in tandem but preclude intact part disassembly (case B)
ContradictionsStrategies lead to unintended increases in environmental impacts, or paradoxical outcomesC2C recommendations vs LCA results (case C)
OversightsEmphasis on one strategy disavows other potentially synergistic ones → blind spotsLight-weighting vs resource efficiency / high virgin material use (case D)

The four cases (Table 3, 6, 8)

CaseProduct / yearApproachMain dilemma observed
AJoy task chair, 2006Efficiency-led (plastic replaces plywood; snap fits)Tensions: efficient assembly precludes easy manual disassembly (co-moulded foam/plastic hard to separate); plywood had a lower carbon impact and LCA score
BCors visitor chair, 2007Durability-ledHierarchies: nylon seat moulded over steel frame → durable and recyclable, but manual disassembly sacrificed
CAra task chair, 2009Efficiency-led, C2C certification (aluminium, “most environmental chair”)Contradictions: LCA shows higher impact than cases A and D; aluminium needs extra polishing energy, casting outsourced to Far East
DDo task chair, 2012Twinned approach: durability + light-weighting (18% material reduction vs case A, FEA)Oversights: lighter but more virgin / less recycled content; PET grades of different melting temperatures hurt recyclability

Operational / tactical / strategic decision levels

  • Operational: ecodesign tools, projects, single strategies, single indicators.
  • Tactical: ecodesign management processes (goals in the design brief, early-stage LCA, systematic NPD review, multiple KPIs).
  • Strategic: business model, firm strategy, external collaborators (e.g. leasing / remanufacturing, PSS).

A unified approach combining the three is needed to manage all types of dilemmas. The business model contextualises ecodesign decisions.

Four phases of the firm’s evolution

PhaseLevelCharacteristics
1. ExploringOperationalInspired but ad-hoc; efficiency measures
2. EvolvingTacticalLearnings internalised; criteria in the design brief; carbon footprinting for external communication
3. EmbeddingStrategicScaling across portfolio; early LCA systematically; multiple indicators; C2C principles internalised
4. UnifyingOperational + tactical + strategicEcodesign strategy and business model perceived as co-dependent

Exemple de l’article — why the business model matters

In case C the firm set out to design its “most environmental chair” through C2C. LCA then found high impact in the context of a direct-sales business model, where multiple product life cycles are not proactively managed by the firm.

Points à vérifier dans l’article

  • Table 8 (case D) says aluminium impacts were “identified through LCA of case B”, but the aluminium chair is case C in the text.
  • Table 6 says case D has the “highest mass of virgin material of all four products”; section 4.4.2 only compares D with case A (lower recycled, higher virgin than A) while D still has the lowest environmental impacts (Table 2).
06ArticleDesign thinking according to Brown (2008)

Design thinking (Brown)

A discipline that uses the designer’s sensibility and methods to match people’s needs with what is technologically feasible and with what a viable business strategy can convert into customer value and market opportunity. Based on direct observation of what people want and need.

The design thinker’s personality profile: empathy · integrative thinking (not only analytical “either/or”) · optimism · experimentalism · collaboration.

Three spaces of a project (not a linear process): inspiration (problem or opportunity that motivates the search) → ideation (generating, developing and testing ideas) → implementation (path to market). Projects loop back, especially through the first two.

Prototypes should take only the time, effort and investment needed to generate feedback; the more “finished” it looks, the less likely people are to give feedback.

Example (Brown)Key facts
Kaiser PermanenteNurse shift changes in 4 hospitals: 45 min at the nurses’ station to get information; prototype (software + procedures) at the patient’s bedside; interval between a nurse’s arrival and first patient interaction more than halved
Shimano (2004)Why do 90% of US adults not ride bikes? Intimidation by cycling culture → “Coasting” bike concept, retail strategy, brand, PR campaign; launched 2007; 7 more manufacturers in 2008
Aravind Eye CarePoverty and distance as creative springboard; lenses made locally: US$4 a pair instead of US$200 in the West (systemic solution)
Bank of America “Keep the Change” (2005)Behaviour insight (saving coins in a jar) → debit purchases rounded up, difference saved; bank matches 100% in first 3 months, then 5% (up to US$250)

Tips box: start at the beginning · take a human-centred approach · prototype early and often · seek outside help · combine big and small projects · budget at the pace of innovation · look for talent wherever possible · design for the cycle.
