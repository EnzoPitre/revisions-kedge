Idée centrale

Money received at different dates cannot be compared directly. To compare “apples with apples”, we bring every amount to the same point in time: forward (future value) or backward (present value).

01NotionWhy money has a time value
  • Inflation: in 5 years, the same €100 may buy fewer goods.
  • Investment: €100 today invested 1 year at 10% becomes €110. The extra €10 is the return (here, the interest).

Exemple du cours

A generous uncle offers €100: today or in 5 years. Most people choose today.

Definitions

  • Future value (FV): the value that a current amount of money will have at a future date after earning interest.
  • Present value (PV): the value today of an amount of money to be received or paid in the future.

With 10%: €100 today → €110 in one year (FV). €100 received in one year is worth \(100/1.10 \approx 90.90\) today (PV).

02NotionWhy interest rates exist
  • Impatience (“myopia”): people prefer to use money today; the future is valued less than the present.
  • Opportunity cost of waiting: a lender who lends €100 cannot use it. The interest compensates this wait (lend €100, get €110 → €10 of interest).
  • Inflation: higher inflation → the lender requires a higher rate.

Fisher effect

Nominal interest rate = real interest rate + expected inflation.

Exemple du cours

Real return wanted 4%, expected inflation 6% → nominal rate of about 10%.

Simple interestCompound interest
Interest is…not reinvested (spent)reinvested: interest earns interest

Where usedspecific contexts: trust funds, charitable organizations, university endowments (principal untouched, only the income is spent)the usual way financial markets work

Future value (compound)

\[ FV = PV \times (1+i)^n \]

Example du cours : \( 100 \times 1.10^3 = 133.10 \). The exponent is the number of periods.

Present value

\[ PV = \dfrac{FV}{(1+i)^n} \]

Example du cours : \( 121 / 1.10^2 = 100 \).

Banks use present value calculations to set loans: the payments happen in the future, but they must be related to the amount lent today.

04MéthodeTimelines & multiple cash flows

Method

  1. Draw a timeline (year 0, 1, 2, 3…).
  2. Move each cash flow separately to the chosen date (compound it forward or discount it back).
  3. Add the results.

Exemple du cours — future value

€100 today and €100 in year 1, rate 10%, value in year 2: \(100\times1.10^2 = 121\) and \(100\times1.10 = 110\) → €231.

The interest earned on the first €100 is reinvested (compound interest). The second €100 earns interest for one year only.

Exemple du cours — present value

€100 in year 1 and €100 in year 2, rate 10%: \(100/1.10 = 90.90\) and \(100/1.10^2 = 82.64\) → €173.54.

With €173.54 invested at 10% today, you can pay €100 in year 1 and €100 in year 2.

The amounts don’t have to be equal (€60, €42, €100…). Each has a different value depending on when it is received: this is the common reference point for CFOs and finance teams evaluating projects.

05NotionAnnuities

Annuity

Equal (constant) cash flows at regular intervals. Most loans are annuities: mortgage loans, business loans, many standard bank loans.

Notation in the course: A (or a) = regular payment, i = interest rate, n = number of periods. Spreadsheet names: PVA (present value of an annuity), FVA (future value of an annuity), PMT (payment).

Conversions (course method)

\[ APR = \text{periodic rate} \times \text{number of periods per year} \]

\[ EAR = (1+\text{periodic rate})^{m} - 1 \quad (m = \text{periods per year}) \]

EAR method of the professor: borrow €1 for the 12 months (or 365 days) at the periodic rate, compute the future value (compounding), subtract the €1 borrowed.

Exemple du cours

Credit card with a daily rate of 0.05%: APR = 0.05% × 365 = 18.25%. In the US, card rates above 15% a year were common; a card announcing “15% APR” may really cost about 17% (“a couple of percentage points more”).

Pièges

The APR is not what you pay. When a problem gives an APR with monthly payments, use APR/12 as the periodic rate and years × 12 as n.

Incohérence dans les documents

The “EAR” spreadsheet shows a periodic rate of 0.1% but its statement says 0.05% daily. The expected answer is therefore unclear. Computed with the statement (0.05%): EAR = 1.0005365 − 1 ≈ 20.02%. With 0.1% it would be ≈ 44.03%. The lecture transcript is also partly garbled on this topic; only the clear parts are kept here.

07ApplicationAmortization schedule

Method (for each year / period)

  1. PMT from the loan amount (PVA), the rate and n (Excel VPM).
  2. Interest paid = beginning balance × rate.
  3. Principal paid = payment − interest paid.
  4. Ending balance = beginning balance − principal paid (= next period’s beginning balance). Last ending balance = 0.
