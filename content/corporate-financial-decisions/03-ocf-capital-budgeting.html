Idée centrale

Determining cash flows is “the most important and difficult task in capital budgeting”. Only incremental, after-tax cash flows matter — not accounting profit.

OCF = EBIT − Taxes + Depreciation and FCF = OCF − Capex − ΔNWC.

01RévisionThe statement of cash flows

Net income mixes accrual revenues and expenses, so it rarely equals the cash generated. The cash flow statement reclassifies every cash movement into three buckets:

BucketContentMeaning
Operating (CFO)Collections from customers · payments to suppliers & employees · interest paid/received, taxes paidThe engine of the business — day-to-day cash generation
Investing (CFI)Purchases of PP&E / capex · sales of PP&E or land · purchases & sales of financial investmentsBuilding (or shrinking) the firm’s productive capacity
Financing (CFF)New borrowings & loan repayments · share issuance & buybacks · dividends paidHow the firm is funded — debt, equity, and payouts

Direct vs indirect method — same answer, two routes

  • Direct: lists actual cash receipts and payments (needs access to the cash account). Optional under IFRS / US GAAP, but firms using it must still disclose the indirect reconciliation.
  • Indirect: starts from net income and adjusts for non-cash items — presented in the course as the mandatory presentation under IFRS & US GAAP.

Indirect method

CFO = Net income + Depreciation − Non-op. gains + Non-op. losses − ΔOperating assets + ΔOperating liabilities

02FormulesOperating cash flow & free cash flow

OCF (project-level formula)

\[ OCF = EBIT - Taxes + Depreciation \]

Start from operating profit, remove cash taxes, add back the non-cash depreciation charge.

FCF (firm-level formula) = Cash flow from assets

\[ FCF = OCF - Capex - \Delta NWC \]

Cash left after reinvesting in fixed assets and working capital — available to repay debt and reward shareholders. (In the notes: “change in net operating working capital”.)

The same logic used to evaluate a single project scales up to a whole company.

03PrincipesProject evaluation: which cash flows count?

Definitions

  • Project evaluation: applying one or more capital budgeting decision rules to the estimated relevant project cash flows to make the investment decision.
  • Relevant cash flows: the incremental cash flows associated with the decision to invest in a project — any and all changes in the firm’s future cash flows that are a direct consequence of taking the project.
  • Stand-alone principle: evaluating a project based on its incremental cash flows; view each project as a “mini-firm” with its own assets, revenues and costs. If projects interact, they can be bunched together.
IssueRule
Only cash flowEstimate on an incremental basis (how the project changes the firm’s cash flows — especially relevant to depreciation); always after tax
Sunk costsDo not include: past, irreversible outflows, not affected by the accept/reject decision
Opportunity costsInclude
Side effectsInclude all incidental effects
Net working capitalInclude: new projects often require cash, inventories and receivables; often recovered when the project ends
Financing costsNot counted (interest, principal on debt, dividends): the discount rate takes care of them

Key issues listed: when is a cash flow incremental · terminology: A. sunk costs, B. opportunity costs, C. side effects, D. net working capital, E. financing costs, F. other issues.

Capital expenditure decisions listed: expand an existing product line · working capital · refunding · leasing · merger and acquisition · enter a new line of business · replacement · advertising campaign · R&D · education and training.

04MéthodeSpecific rules for cash flow accounting

1. Initial outlay

  • (−) Cost of the asset (installed)
  • (−) Additional working capital investments
  • (+) Cash flow from sale of an old asset (replacement situation):
    • Selling price > book value: tax = (selling price − book value) × tax rate; CF = selling price − tax
    • Selling price < book value: tax concession = (book value − selling price) × tax rate; CF = selling price + tax concession

2. Intermediate cash flows (operations)

  • (+) Added revenues · (−) increased expenses, including overhead · (+) savings: labor and material
  • (+) Depreciation expense (add back)
  • Do not include interest expense.

3. Terminal cash flow (closing the business)

  • (+) Sale of fixed assets (after the tax effect above)
  • (+) Recapture of working capital

Depreciation

  • Straight line: a fixed percentage of the asset base.
  • Modified ACRS (MACRS): the 1986 Tax Reform Act allows firms to “front-load” depreciation charges.
MACRS classProperty
3-yearEquipment used in research
5-yearAutos, computers
7-yearMost industrial equipment (furniture)
10-yearCertain long-lived types of equipment
27.5-yearResidential rental real property, including commercial and industrial buildings

Land cannot be depreciated.

The 27.5-year class is described as “residential rental real property, including commercial and industrial buildings”, and the 31.5-year class also lists commercial and industrial buildings. The notes do not resolve this; check with the professor before relying on it.

05ExempleNorma Desmond Enterprises

Exemple du cours — data

  1. Machine costs $21,000, depreciated over 7 years straight-line. After 3 years, resale value expected $15,000.
  2. Additional sales: 10,000 units/year at $5/unit.
  3. Variable cost $3/unit; fixed costs $5,000/year; project life 3 years.
  4. Investment in net working capital: $10,000.
  5. Required return (cost of capital) 20%; tax rate 34%.

Tax on sale (shown in the course: −1,020): the machine is sold above book value (book value after 3 years = 21,000 − 3 × 3,000 = 12,000), so tax = (15,000 − 12,000) × 34% = 1,020.

Step 4 — Evaluation

\[ NPV = -31{,}000 + \frac{10{,}920}{1.20} + \frac{10{,}920}{1.20^2} + \frac{34{,}900}{1.20^3} = 5{,}880 \]

IRR = 29.5% > 20% → NPV > 0 and IRR > required return → the firm should invest (decision derived from the course rules; the notes leave the “Why or why not?” question open).
