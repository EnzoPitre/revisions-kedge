Idée centrale

The course follows one logic, session after session: understand the consumer first, then see how brands turn that understanding into a unique, memorable experience (CB focus → CEM focus).

02DéfinitionWhat is consumer behaviour?

Consumer Behaviour (CB)

The study of the processes involved when individuals or groups select, purchase, use or dispose of products, services, ideas or experiences to satisfy needs and desires and to define and express their identities.

An inherently interdisciplinary field: it explains not just what consumers buy but why.

DisciplineContributionLimit (slide)
EconomicsUtility & rationalityIgnores social/qualitative factors
AnthropologyRituals, myths & symbolsOverlooks ethics
Psychology (most influential in CB)Decision-making, motivation, perception, learning, the self and personality: the individual mindLeaves the social context out
Sociology (growing influence, notably Consumer Culture Theory)Social forces: family, communities, tribes, sub/counterculturesCaptures the collective dynamics psychology cannot

03PourquoiWhy study CB & link with marketing

Key message

If you don’t understand your audience, you can market what you perceive as the best product ever — and still not sell it.

Exemple du cours — The New Coke story

1975: the Pepsi Challenge. 1985: New Coke (“Change for the best!”) → back to Coca-Cola Classic just 79 days later. 2019: Stranger Things (season 3): Coca-Cola turns its worst year into a pop-culture moment.

Marketing (American Marketing Association)

An organizational function and a set of processes for creating, communicating and delivering value to customers and for managing customer relationships in ways that benefit the organization and its stakeholders. The study of consumers is at the core.

04TendancesTrends that shape consumer behaviour today
TrendKey ideas
Symbolic consumptionPeople often buy brands not for what they do but for what they mean. Brands build entire symbolic universes (“branded world”: story, aesthetics, ritual). McCracken’s Meaning Transfer Model (1986): meaning moves from culture → to the brand (advertising, design) → to the consumer (how they use, wear, talk about it). Consumers choose brands consistent with their needs, personality and sense of self
Pop cultureMusic, movies, sports, books, celebrities consumed by the mass market. Meets brands at different depths: celebrity endorsement · product placement → branded entertainment · merchandise from strong creative/cultural brands. Marketing doesn’t just borrow from pop culture — it should learn FROM it
Social mediaShift from one-way messaging to a “horizontal revolution”. Redistributes trust (user-generated content shapes perceptions as much as advertising) and compresses the decision process (discovery, evaluation, purchase in one flow)
Data analyticsMassive consumer data (transactions, GPS, clicks, social activity) analysed by algorithms → turns behaviour into a resource; marketing moves from reactive to anticipatory
Artificial intelligenceMachine-learning systems inside everyday interactions. Personalizes the choice environment at scale: many small choices are pre-filtered by algorithms → how much of what we “choose” is still our own?

05ProcessusThe consumer decision-making process (CDMP)

5 stages: need recognition → search for information → pre-purchase evaluation of alternatives → product choice, purchase & consumption → post-consumption evaluation. Because some decisions matter more than others, the effort differs:

TypeDescriptionExample (slide)
Cognitive (rational)Sequential, weighing pros and cons: the full process step by stepLaptop
HabitualSkips search & evaluation: “I just do what I usually do”; the decision was made by past behaviourBaguette
AffectiveA “gut” decision based on emotional reaction (enthusiasm, joy, even disgust)“I ♥ Apple”

A few nuances

  • Heuristics guide fast thinking: price, brand name and country of origin are quick signals of quality (mental shortcuts replacing research).
  • Loyalty or just inertia? Repurchasing can reflect genuine loyalty or simply ease; identical from the outside but they call for very different marketing responses.
  • Context effects: framing, priming and nudging (coup de pouce) shape decisions without restricting them: they change how choices are presented, not which choices exist.
  • Online decision-making: the challenge flipped from finding enough information to filtering the excess; reviews, comparison sites and cybermediaries (portals, AI programs) help.

Three forces behind every decision

Motivation (acts at need recognition)

The processes that lead people to behave as they do. It occurs when a need is activated, creating a state of tension the consumer wants to reduce. A need reflects a basic goal: utilitarian (functional/practical benefit) or hedonic (experiential: emotion, fantasy).

Desired state vs actual state (the gap creates tension) → drive (degree of arousal, the force that makes a person respond) → new actual state closer to the desired state (the “goal”).

Involvement (acts on search and evaluation)

A motivational variable: the personal relevance of an object or activity, based on inherent needs, values and interest (Laurent & Kapferer, 1985; Zaichkowsky, 1985). It shapes the amount of information search, perceived differences among alternatives, deliberation time, and perceived value and price sensitivity.

Continuum (level of motivation): inertia → willingness to listen → interest in the product → active search for information → total commitment & passion. Low = routine purchases of generic products; high = identification with a brand to the point of obsession.

Attitude (acts on pre-purchase evaluation)

A lasting, general evaluation of people (including oneself), objects, advertisements or issues; anything one has an attitude toward is an attitude object (Ao).

ABC model: Affect (how the consumer feels) · Behavior (intention to act) · Cognition (what they believe true).

Attitudes seek internal harmony. When A, B and C fall out of sync → cognitive dissonance (Festinger, 1957), a tension we resolve by adjusting one of the three. Exemple du cours: “I know smoking causes cancer” vs “I smoke” → quit, deny the risk or downplay it.

06ExpérienceWhat is an experience?

Today people consume mainly to exist (identity) and not only to live (needs): consumption produces meanings (symbolic dimension) and must provoke sensations and emotions touching the search for identity. Consumers are increasingly seen as emotional beings seeking experiences (slide images: UTMB, Disneyland).

Holbrook & Hirschman’s experiential paradigm (1982)

The notion of “consumer experience” emerged with their article The experiential aspects of consumption: consumer fantasies, feelings and fun. An experience is not the product itself but the subjective, conscious state it generates: a flow of fantasies, feelings and fun built from symbolic meaning, hedonic response and esthetic judgment, rather than purely rational problem-solving.

The old view: information processingThe experiential paradigm
Consumer = logical thinker · solves problems to make a choice · decides on performance/utilitarian characteristicsFantasies, feelings & fun · subjective state of consciousness · symbolic meanings, hedonic responses, aesthetic criteria · a psycho-emotional axis

The paradigm enriches the rational model rather than replacing it.

Holbrook & Corfman (1985)

Person × Object × Situation = a subjective, singular experience. The consumer is the true producer of their own experience.

Exemple du cours

Little Monsters × Lady Gaga’s performance × Sydney Town Hall = a euphoric communion between artist and fans, unrepeatable outside that night.

Experience is everywhere (Filser, 2002; Carù & Cova, 2006): companies shape it through the Situation (space, staging) and the Object itself.

Exemples du cours

  • Apple Store: open tables for hands-on testing, “Today at Apple” workshops; an iPhone designed to feel minimal, tactile, desirable.
  • Lush: fully staged sensory store (live demonstrations, scent, touch); cosmetics designed to smell, feel and look irresistible.
  • Eataly: Italian marketplace where you shop, eat and learn; fresh focaccia and wood-fired pizza make the product the experience.

Extraordinary experiences

A “special class of hedonic consumption activities” (Arnould & Price, 1993) sharing three features (Scott, Cayla & Cova, 2017):

  • Intensity — sensorially and emotionally rich; consumers lose themselves in it.
  • Engagement — drags consumers in through a socially constructed frame.
  • Temporality — time-limited; returning to everyday life can create disillusion.

Exemples du cours (research settings): rafting (Arnould & Price 1993), Tough Mudder (Scott et al. 2017), cosplay (Seregina & Weijo 2017), LARP (Orazi & Van Laer 2023).

07ModèlesExperiential marketing & customer experience management

Experiential marketing

Goes beyond satisfying functional and tangible needs: it answers the consumers’ quest for immersive and diverse experiences to explore new meanings and escape daily life. Companies evolve from producers of goods, then services, to “producers of experiences”. Experience = competitive advantage to differentiate offers (value) and build a strong relationship (CRM); emotions determine how experiences are valued. The consumer is not passive but a producer of their own experiences.

Pine & Gilmore — The Experience Economy: four realms

Two axes: passive ↔ active participation and absorption ↔ immersion.

RealmDescriptionExamples
EntertainmentMore passive participation; connection = absorptionAttending a concert
EducationalMore active participation, but customers remain more outside the event than immersedA wine-tasting course
EscapistGreater immersion: both active participation and immersionActing in a play, playing in an orchestra, descending the Grand Canyon
EstheticImmersed in an environment but little or no effect on itViewing the Grand Canyon from the rim; a visitor in an art gallery

Carù & Cova — Paths to experience (2007)

Creating an experience offer: product = experience jointly built by the company and the consumer. Differentiating through experience — four levers (as labelled on the slide): the product itself → consumer-driven (Stanley) · communication → company-driven (Nespresso) · distribution → co-driven (Starbucks) · consumer participation → consumer-driven (LEGO Ideas).

Customer experience management (CEM)

Schmitt (1999, 2003)

CEM = the process of strategically managing a customer’s entire experience with a product or company. Centered on touchpoint analysis: identify every point of contact with the organization, brand, service or product, then design the journey the company wants the customer to experience. Moves beyond satisfaction and CRM: every interaction is a chance to create meaning. Contribution: a micro-level, actionable framework.

5-step framework (slide text): (1) analyzing the customer’s experiential world · (2) building an experiential platform · (3) designing the brand experience · (4) structuring the customer interface · (5) committing to continuous innovation.

Écart dans la slide

The diagram on the same slide shows a cycle of 6 nodes with different wording: assess needs & segment customers · map the journey for customer segments · identify the desired experience · design the brand experience · structure the customer touchpoints · measure & develop. The text says 5 steps; both appear in the slides.

Lemon & Verhoef — mapping the customer journey (2016)

Experience is built across the whole journey and every point of contact.

08ProjetGroup project (Poster Fair)
  • Groups of 4–5, already assigned and fixed; work together from fieldwork to the final poster and video.
  • Choose an experience: a store, brand, company or organization with a specific customer experience — or a truly extraordinary experience (slide examples: IKEA, Nature & Découvertes).
  • Go into the field: observe (space, staging, details), interview managers (how it is designed and run) and customers (how it is lived). Fieldwork must answer: 1) what experience is offered? 2) which consumer behaviour concepts does it rely on? 3) how is it managed on the brand’s side?
  • Deliverables due for Session 5: a poster (only support at the Poster Fair; 5–10 min, poster only, no slides; Q&A) and a video of 4–5 min sent by email (shows the in-store experience, with prior authorization, and your interviews).
  • Evaluation: understanding of the theory and concepts · coherence & structure of the analysis · creativity. = 40% of the final grade.
